Plant basics for your new pot

A quick note first: we sell pots, not plants. But a lovely empty planter is a bit sad, so here's the friendly-neighbor version of everything you need to fill it and keep the green bit alive. Get these three things roughly right and you're most of the way there.

Before you plant anything, check it's safe for your household. Lots of common houseplants — pothos, lilies, aloe, philodendron, snake plant and others — are toxic to cats and dogs if chewed, and a few bother curious kids too. Look your specific plant up against a pet-safe list (the ASPCA keeps a good one) before it goes on a reachable shelf. Herbs like basil and mint, spider plants, and calatheas are easy, safe places to start.

1. Light — match the plant to your window

The single most common mistake is buying a plant your room can't feed. Before you shop for the green part, read your light honestly:

  • Bright, direct sun (a south-facing sill that gets hours of full light): great for herbs — basil, thyme, rosemary — and succulents.
  • Bright but indirect (near a window but out of the beam): the comfort zone for most houseplants, including pothos, calatheas, and peace lilies.
  • Low light (an interior corner, a north window): reach for the tough survivors — snake plants, ZZ plants, pothos will tolerate it too, just slower.

If you're not sure, hold your hand a foot above the spot at midday. A crisp shadow means bright light; a soft, fuzzy shadow means medium; barely any shadow means low.

2. Water — less often than you think

More houseplants die from too much water than too little. The trick is to water thoroughly, then wait.

  • Check before you pour. Push a finger an inch into the soil. Damp? Wait. Dry? Water.
  • Water until it drains. Give enough that a little runs out the bottom — that means the whole root ball drank, not just the top.
  • Empty the saucer. Roots sitting in standing water rot. Tip out whatever collects in the saucer after a few minutes.
  • Herbs are thirstier than most foliage plants, especially in a sunny window. Basil in particular likes to stay evenly moist — but still not soggy.

3. Drainage — the part the pot handles

This is where the right pot earns its keep. Roots need air as well as water, and a drainage hole is what lets the excess escape instead of drowning them. Our Stoneware Planter has a real drainage hole and a matching saucer for exactly this reason.

A few ways to set it up:

  • Plant straight into the pot with a good potting mix, letting water run through the hole into the saucer.
  • Use a nursery pot inside a decorative cover pot with no hole — then lift the inner pot out to water it at the sink and let it drain fully before returning it. Never let a plant sit in a hole-less pot full of water.
  • Don't rely on "just a layer of rocks." Gravel at the bottom of a hole-less pot doesn't replace real drainage — a hole does the job far better.

Good beginner pairings for our pots

  • Sunny sill: a pot of basil or a little herb trio. Useful, forgiving, and safe if a kid nibbles.
  • Bright shelf, has a pet: a spider plant or a calathea — both pet-safe and easy on the eyes.
  • Dim corner: a snake plant. Nearly unkillable and architectural in a good stoneware pot.
