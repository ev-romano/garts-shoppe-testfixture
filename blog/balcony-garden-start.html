How to start a balcony garden (from someone who started on one)

From the Journal · a note from Garth

Gart's Shoppe started on a balcony roughly the size of a bath mat. Three pots, a bag of soil hauled up two flights, and more optimism than sense. Most of what I know about making a small outdoor space feel alive, I learned by getting it wrong out there first. So if you're staring at a bare rail or a slab of grey concrete wondering where to begin — here's the friend-with-good-taste version, minus the mistakes I made.

Start by reading your light

Before you buy a single thing, spend a day noticing where the sun actually lands. Balconies lie to you. The corner you assumed was sunny might get an hour at noon and nothing else. Watch for where light sits longest — that's your prime real estate. South and west faces get the most; north-facing balconies are shadier but perfectly good for leafy greens and low-light-tolerant plants. Match your plants to the light you have, not the light you wish you had. This one habit saves more plants than anything else.

Begin small, and buy the right thing once

The temptation is to fill the whole space on day one. Don't. Three good pots beat a dozen flimsy ones that crack by August. A sturdy planter with real drainage and a saucer will still be with you in five years, holding a different plant each season, while the bargain plastic ones go brittle in the sun and split. Start with a couple of pieces you genuinely like looking at, then add as you learn what thrives. A little joy in the pot itself matters — you'll tend a garden you find beautiful far more faithfully than a beige one.

Grow what you'll use

The balcony garden that sticks around is the one that earns its keep. Herbs are the perfect starter crop: basil, mint, thyme, and chives are forgiving, fast, and there's real satisfaction in snipping dinner from your own rail. They also happen to be safe if a curious pet or kid samples a leaf — worth knowing before you plant. A tomato in a big pot, a few strawberries, a pot of flowers just because they make you happy on a grey morning: all fair game. Useful first, joy close behind.

Drainage is not optional

If I could go back and tell balcony-me one thing, it's this: roots need to breathe. A pot without a drainage hole is a slow way to drown a plant. Use pots that drain, sit them in saucers so you're not staining the concrete or annoying the neighbour below, and empty the saucers after a good soak. When you water, water thoroughly and then leave it alone until the top inch of soil dries. More balcony plants die from kindness — daily little splashes — than from neglect.

Mind the wind and the heat

Balconies are windier and hotter than the ground-level gardens most advice is written for. Wind dries pots out fast and can topple tall, light ones — which is another quiet argument for a heavier stoneware pot that stays put. On a scorching afternoon, a dark corner can bake; group pots together so they shade each other's soil and hold moisture a little longer.

Give it a season

Your first balcony garden will teach you more than any guide. Something will bolt in the heat, something will sulk in the shade, and something you almost didn't buy will take over in the best way. That's the whole joy of it. Keep the pieces that last, move plants around until they're happy, and let the space become yours over a season rather than a weekend.

That bath-mat balcony is why Gart's exists — the belief that a small space, tended with a bit of care and a few good things, can genuinely change how a day feels.
