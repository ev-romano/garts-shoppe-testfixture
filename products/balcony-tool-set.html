What's in it

  • Trowel — for digging, scooping soil, and planting. The workhorse.
  • Transplanter — narrower and slightly scooped, for moving seedlings and settling a plant into a fresh pot without wrecking the roots.
  • Snips — for deadheading, harvesting herbs, and trimming back a leggy corner.

What they're made of

The heads are forged steel — one solid piece, not stamped from thin sheet metal that bends the first time you hit a root. The handles are ash, the same wood used for tool handles for centuries because it's strong and shock-absorbing and feels good in the hand. Each tool is full-tang: the steel runs through the handle, so the head can't wobble loose. These are tools you grip and feel the difference immediately.

Built to be sharpened and kept

This is the whole point. Cheap garden tools are designed to be thrown away — thin steel, riveted plastic handles, no way to maintain them. Ours are built the old way, so you can keep them. Hone the trowel edge and the snips with a whetstone when they dull. Wipe the steel and rub a little oil into the ash at season's end. Do that and this set outlives a dozen blister-pack sets from the garden center. Buy the right thing once.

"Do I really need a set — or just a trowel?"

Fair question, and we won't hard-sell you. If all you'll ever do is scoop soil into a pot, a single trowel is genuinely enough. The set earns itself the moment you go past that — the first time you move a seedling without mangling it (transplanter), or snip fresh basil for dinner (snips) instead of tearing it. Most balcony and small-yard gardeners reach for all three within the first month. At $68 the three together cost less than buying good versions separately, and they're made to match. But if you truly only need one tool, buy one tool. That's honest advice, not a script.

Is $68 worth it?

Compared to a $15 big-box three-pack, yes — because you'll buy that pack again next spring, and the spring after. Forged steel and ash cost more up front and then stop costing you. Think of it as the last garden tool set you buy, because for most people it is.

Care & use

Knock off the soil and dry the steel after use so it doesn't rust. A wipe of oil on the blades and handles a few times a season keeps everything happy. Store them out of standing rain. When an edge gets dull, a few passes on a whetstone brings it back — that's a feature, not a chore. Full care notes are in the help center.

Safety note

The snips are genuinely sharp — that's what makes them good, and what makes them worth keeping away from small kids. Store the set up high or in a closed drawer.
